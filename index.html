User guide

Quick start

SatTrail predicts which satellites will cross your telescope's field of view during an imaging session. It shows how many sub-exposures they would spoil, and whether a different start time or exposure length would do better.

  1. Enter your location, target, telescope, exposure and session times.
  2. Check that the satellite data box holds orbit data. It fills in by itself.
  3. Tap Run simulation and wait for the progress bar to finish.
  4. Read the sky view and efficiency, then tap rows in the tables to compare other start times and exposure lengths.

Tap the ? beside any heading to jump to help for that part.

Location

What to enter

Latitude and longitude in decimal degrees. North latitudes and east longitudes are positive; south and west are negative. Example: 42.60 and -5.57. Or tap Use current GPS and allow location access when your iPad asks.

What the app does

It uses your position to work out where every satellite and your target appear in your sky. Being off by a few hundred meters makes no practical difference. A Wi-Fi-only iPad estimates its position from the network and can be off by more, so type the coordinates of your observing spot if you know them. Height above sea level isn't needed.

Target

What to enter

  • Common target: pick a Messier object and the app fills in azimuth and altitude for you. Leave it on Manual entry to type your own values.
  • Azimuth: 0 to 360 degrees, measured clockwise from north. 0 is north, 90 east, 180 south, 270 west.
  • Altitude: degrees above the horizon, from 0 at the horizon to 90 straight overhead.
  • Target width and height: the object's apparent size in arcminutes (60 arcminutes is 1 degree). Rough examples: Orion Nebula 85 by 60, M13 20 by 20, Andromeda 190 by 60.

Azimuth and altitude are the target's position at the moment your session starts. A planetarium app or your telescope's app will show them for any object.

What the app does

Picking a Messier object works out its azimuth and altitude for the location and start time currently entered, so set those first. If you change either afterwards, pick the object again. If it sits below the horizon at your start time, the app tells you.

The app then turns your azimuth and altitude into a fixed spot among the stars and assumes your mount keeps tracking that spot for the whole session. The target stays centered while the sky turns around it. Width and height only size the gray oval on the sky view; they don't change any results.

Telescope and exposure

What to enter

Choose your telescope. The field-of-view boxes fill in with the published width and height in degrees, rounded. They stay editable: choose Custom or change the numbers if you use a different lens, mode or crop. If your telescope's own app reports a different field of view, trust the app and enter it here.

Choose an exposure from 10 seconds to 5 minutes. This is the length of one sub-exposure.

What the app does

The width is treated as the horizontal side of your picture and the height as the vertical side, with the camera level to the horizon. The app splits your session into back-to-back frames of your exposure length. A frame counts as rejected if any satellite is inside your field of view at any moment while that frame is exposing.

You can change the exposure after a run by tapping a row in the Exposure length table. There's no need to run again.

Time window

What to enter

The start and end of the imaging session, using the date and time controls. Times are in your iPad's local time zone. The end must be after the start. Use the window you actually plan to shoot rather than the whole night, and pick times after dark: the app warns you when the Sun is up or the sky is still in twilight.

What the app does

It simulates exactly that window. It also looks 65 minutes before and after so the start-time shift table has data to compare. Only whole frames count; any leftover time shorter than one exposure is ignored. Longer windows take longer to compute.

Satellite data

What it is

Orbit data, called TLEs, lets the app calculate where each satellite is at any moment. The box fills automatically with CelesTrak's list of active satellites, about 15,000 objects including Starlink, OneWeb, Kuiper and Qianfan. It downloads once a day and the app remembers it in your browser. Refresh satellite data forces a new download. The line beside the button says what happened.

Format if you paste your own

Three lines per satellite: a name, then a line starting with 1 and a line starting with 2 . Blank lines are ignored, and you can paste any number of satellites. Fewer satellites run faster.

SATELLITE NAME
1 NNNNNU ...
2 NNNNN ...

If the download fails

You may be offline, or the request may be blocked. The status line says so and your existing data stays in the box. CelesTrak only updates its data every two hours, so refreshing more often returns a notice instead of data; the app recognizes that and keeps what you have. You can also paste data from celestrak.org. Orbits change over time, so data older than about a week gets less reliable. Refresh before an important session.

Running the simulation

What happens

Tap Run simulation. The app checks every satellite in the data box in two passes. A quick coarse scan finds satellites that come near your target. Then a fine check at half-second steps measures the paths and timing of just those. The progress bar shows how many satellites are done. With the full list this can take a minute or more, so keep the page open.

Only satellites above your horizon and lit by the Sun are counted. A satellite inside Earth's shadow leaves no streak, so the app skips it. Results appear below when the scan finishes. If you change the location, target, telescope, time window or satellite data, run again. Tapping table rows doesn't need a new run.

Sky view

What you're seeing

A patch of sky centered on your target, 2.4 times wider and taller than your field of view. The scales run along the border: the horizontal scale is azimuth offset from the target and the vertical scale is altitude offset, both in degrees.

  • The blue rectangle is your telescope's field of view, in true proportion for the scope you chose.
  • The gray oval is your target's approximate size and position.
  • Each line is one sunlit satellite's path across this patch during the time window shown. If a satellite drops into Earth's shadow partway across, its line stops there.

Red: passes through the central area of your frame, the inner half in each direction, where your target usually sits.
Orange: crosses your frame but stays out of the center.
Green: passes near but never enters your frame.

The line under the picture counts the trails and states how many frames are rejected at the current exposure. Only red and orange trails reject frames. An amber note appears below the count when the Sun is above your horizon, or the sky is still in twilight, during the window shown.

What you can tap

  • Color buttons: hide or show a color. Hide green to see only the trails that spoil frames.
  • A row in the Start-time shift table: the picture switches to that shifted window.
  • A satellite in the Satellites in frame list: that trail becomes a thick dashed line with its name.
  • A row in the Exposure length table: the counts and frame map update. The paths stay the same, because the same satellites cross whatever your exposure is.

Frame map

The colored strip under the picture has one bar for each sub-exposure in the window. Green frames are kept and red frames are rejected. It redraws whenever you change the exposure or the start time.

Efficiency

What you're seeing

The large percentage is the share of your frames that stay free of satellite trails. Because every frame is the same length, it's also the share of your exposure time you keep. It's green at 90% or more, amber from 75% to 90%, and red below 75%.

  • Session: length of the window being shown.
  • Sub-exposure: the exposure length in use.
  • Frames: how many whole frames fit in the window.
  • Rejected: frames during which a satellite was inside your field of view. Near-misses don't count.

The numbers always describe the scenario currently selected in the tables below: the start time and the exposure you tapped last.

Start-time shift

What you're seeing

Nine rows answer "what if I started earlier or later?" They run from 60 minutes earlier to 60 minutes later in 15-minute steps, and each keeps your session length. Columns show the shift, the new start time, the frames, the rejected frames and the efficiency. Rows are always measured from the start time you entered, using the exposure currently selected.

What you can tap

Tap a row to load that window. The sky view, frame map, efficiency, exposure table and satellite list all update, and the highlighted row shows which window is active. Tap the 0 min row to return to your original plan.

The table doesn't check whether your target is still high enough at the shifted time. Confirm that in your planetarium app before you move your session.

Exposure length

What you're seeing

Eleven rows from 10 seconds to 5 minutes, calculated for the window currently selected. Columns show the exposure, the frames, the rejected frames and the efficiency.

What you can tap

Tap a row to switch to that exposure. The efficiency, the count line, the frame map and the start-time shift table all update. A longer exposure is more likely to catch a satellite and loses more each time, so efficiency usually falls as exposure grows. Shorter subs lose less per hit but give you more frames to store and stack. Balance the table against what your camera needs, such as signal per sub and stacking time.

Satellites in frame

What you're seeing

Every satellite that enters your field of view during the window shown, in time order. Columns show the name, when it enters, when it leaves (your iPad's local time) and how many seconds it stays inside. The dot color matches the sky view. Near-misses aren't listed.

What you can tap

Tap a satellite to trace it on the sky view as a thick dashed line with its name beside it. Tap it again to clear the highlight. Use the times to know when to expect rejected frames.

What the app doesn't do

  • Sunlight: only satellites lit by the Sun are counted, using a simple model of Earth's shadow. A satellite at the very edge of the shadow can be off by a few seconds. The app doesn't decide whether your sky is dark enough to image; it warns you when the Sun is up or the sky is in twilight.
  • Brightness: it doesn't judge whether a faint satellite would show in your subs.
  • Field rotation: it assumes the camera stays level to the horizon and doesn't rotate during the session. Real rotation depends on your mount.
  • Tracking: it assumes perfect tracking of your target.
  • Very fast passes: rarely, an extremely brief pass can slip between the coarse scan steps and go undetected.
  • Orbit accuracy: satellites maneuver and orbits drift, so older data is less reliable.
  • Field of view: the telescope sizes are rounded published values.
