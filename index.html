1. Physical Principles & Orbital Mechanics

This application calculates real-time satellite trajectory intersections with your telescope's optical Field of View (FOV). Understanding why satellite trails behave as they do requires recognizing three key orbital mechanics principles:

ISS Transit Velocity & FOV Scale

The International Space Station (ISS) travels at ~7.66 km/s in Low Earth Orbit (LEO). While a full horizon-to-horizon visible pass across the open sky takes 5 to 6 minutes ($180^\circ$), your smart telescope FOV is extremely small ($1.0^\circ$ to $3.2^\circ$).

Inside a zoomed $7.1^\circ \times 4.0^\circ$ sky context window, the ISS covers $7^\circ$ of arc in approximately 21 seconds (0.33°/sec). A trail crossing duration of 20–25 seconds in this window is physically accurate.

Earth's Shadow Cone (Umbra)

Satellites do not emit light; they are only visible when illuminated by direct sunlight. During deep midnight (e.g. 11:00 PM – 3:30 AM), Earth casts a massive shadow cone into space.

Most LEO satellites enter Earth's shadow and turn dark, creating a prolonged satellite-free window in the middle of the night (highlighted in mint green on the timeline bar).

Spatial Geometry Math: The visible sky hemisphere contains 20,626 square degrees. A $2.95^\circ \times 1.66^\circ$ sensor frame covers only ~4.9 sq deg—representing just 0.024% of the visible sky. Finding 15 to 20 satellite passes in that tiny slice across a full 9-hour night is mathematically normal.

2. Sky View Interface & Boundary Truncation

The Sky View canvas plots the satellite trajectories in differential Azimuth and Altitude offsets ($\Delta\text{Az}, \Delta\text{Alt}$) relative to your celestial target centered at $(0^\circ, 0^\circ)$.

  • Outer Grid Window: Displays a $7.1^\circ \times 4.0^\circ$ sky context view centered on the astronomical object (e.g., Andromeda Galaxy M31).
  • Inner Blue Box: Represents your active smart telescope or camera sensor FOV (scaled dynamically based on your dropdown selection).
  • Red Trails (Through Target): Satellite vectors passing directly through the target center zone.
  • Orange Trails (Across Frame): Satellite vectors crossing inside the blue telescope FOV box without hitting the central target core.
  • Purple Trails (Near-Miss): Satellite vectors passing outside the blue FOV box but inside the surrounding $7.1^\circ \times 4.0^\circ$ sky view. (Purple was selected to contrast sharply against the green timeline elements!)
  • Vector Edge Truncation: All line vectors are strictly clipped at the exact grid perimeter ($\pm 3.55^\circ$ Az, $\pm 2.0^\circ$ Alt) to prevent rendering bleed past the plot borders.

3. Full Night Timeline & Real-time Filtering

The Full Night Timeline features a dual-handle range slider spanning astronomical twilight from dusk (08:37 PM) to dawn (05:48 AM).

Real-time Synchronous Updates: Adjusting either handle immediately filters active satellites for that specific timeframe, updating the Sky View canvas vectors, efficiency percentages, and satellite active table in real time without refreshing the page.

Mint Green Highlight: The green bar above the strip marks the single longest continuous satellite-free interval tonight (e.g., 10:56 PM – 11:57 PM).

4. Efficiency Metrics & Sub-Exposure Mathematics

Sub-exposure duration (selected via the header dropdown in 5-second steps from 10s to 300s) directly dictates how many frames are corrupted by a satellite pass.

Total Frames = Math.floor(Selected Session Duration / Sub-Exposure Duration)
Corrupted Frames = Count of unique sub-exposure intervals intersected by active satellite passes
Efficiency % = ((Total Frames - Corrupted Frames) / Total Frames) * 100%

5. Deep Sky Target Recommendations (Nebulae, Galaxies)

When shooting deep sky objects through narrow focal length telescopes ($0.5^\circ$ to $3.5^\circ$ FOV):

  1. Twilight Sub-Exposure Strategy: Use 10s to 20s sub-exposures during dusk/dawn peak satellite hours. If a satellite crosses during a 10s sub, you only lose 10 seconds of exposure instead of losing 300 seconds.
  2. Deep Midnight Strategy: Transition to longer sub-exposures (30s to 120s+) during the mint green satellite-free midnight window.
  3. Kappa-Sigma Stacking Rejection: Always enable Kappa-Sigma Clipping, Winsorized Sigma Clipping, or Linear Fit Rejection in your stacking software (Siril, PixInsight, DSS, or built-in smart scope app). Statistical rejection algorithms effortlessly reject trail pixels without degrading image signal-to-noise ratio.

6. Wide-Field & Milky Way Astrophotography Strategy

Wide-angle camera lenses ($14mm$ to $35mm$) cover massive sky regions ($60^\circ$ to $110^\circ+$ FOV).

  • High Density Realities: In wide fields, dozens of satellites cross every few minutes during twilight.
  • Panorama Panel Timing: Shoot wide Milky Way panorama tiles during deep astronomical night (midnight) when satellites are eclipsed in Earth's shadow cone.
  • Median Stacking: Take 5 to 10 sequential sub-exposures per foreground or sky tile and stack using Median integration to eliminate all moving satellite streaks automatically.
